About Me

Near-Surface Geophysics | Hydrogeology | Aquifer Systems | Karst | Drone Mapping | GIS Data Analysis | Enceladus | Titan

My name is Rosario Cecilio-Flores-Elie, and I hold a Master's degree in Geological Sciences from Cornell University. I am a Dragonfly Guest Investigator at the Johns Hopkins Applied Physics Laboratory.

Research Interests

My research focuses on how subsurface water movement is reflected at the surface, using remote sensing and geophysical techniques to track changes over time and distinguish natural from human influences. I also apply these methods to study planetary bodies like Enceladus and Titan.

I have developed skills that connect surface observations with subsurface processes. In the Yucatan Peninsula, I used electrical resistivity to visualize fracture networks in cenotes and compared this data with water conductivity measurements to understand water flow. In the geothermal area of El Tatio in Northern Chile, I used drone photogrammetry to produce precise maps of hydrothermal features. My work on Titan involves analyzing geomorphology around impact craters to infer crustal stresses.

Water-rock interactions are significant on Earth and in icy ocean worlds. Subsurface fluids move through ice, creating discernible surface features, and understanding these requires knowledge of underlying stresses. While no terrestrial location perfectly mirrors icy ocean worlds, fieldwork can provide valuable insight into surface fluid interactions and their structural controls.

Research

My research connects planetary surface observations to the processes that drive them. I study fluid movement through fractured rock and its surface signatures, using remote sensing, GIS mapping, and near-surface geophysics. This work includes hydrothermal systems at El Tatio Geothermal Field in Chile, karst aquifers in the Yucatán Peninsula, and geological mapping on Titan as a NASA Dragonfly Guest Investigator. It builds on my astrophysics master's thesis on TESS light curves, stellar rotation, and planet-moon interactions that drive geological activity on moons across our Solar System and beyond. Together, these projects use Earth as a reference for understanding similar processes on ocean worlds like Enceladus and Titan.

Geysers in El Tatio, Chile

The El Tatio Geothermal Field in northern Chile is the world's third-largest geyser field and one of the highest in elevation. For my master's thesis at Cornell, I investigated how fault and fracture networks influence the location and temperature of hydrothermal features at the surface. By utilizing thousands of drone images, I created optical and thermal orthomosaics and digital elevation models of the Upper and Middle Basins. I mapped structural lineaments and hydrothermal vents in ArcGIS Pro and used Python to analyze relationships among vent locations, temperatures, and the surrounding fault network.

Main findings indicate that a strike-slip fault system significantly shapes hydrothermal activity at El Tatio, with each basin responding differently to the fault network. This research positions El Tatio as a natural laboratory for studying how fractures guide fluids to the surface, which is crucial for understanding plume activity on icy ocean worlds like Enceladus.

Dragonfly Guest Investigator

Polygonal impact craters (PICs) feature straight rim segments rather than circular shapes, as preexisting crustal fractures and faults influence how the crater develops and collapses. This characteristic makes them natural records of crustal stresses and fracture patterns. As a NASA Dragonfly Guest Investigator, I examine PICs on Titan, Saturn's largest moon, in partnership with Dr. Emily Martin at the Smithsonian National Air and Space Museum in Washington, D.C.

Using data from the Cassini mission, I analyze the regional geomorphology around these craters in ArcGIS Pro to infer the stresses that shaped them. Collectively, these craters contribute to a comprehensive understanding of Titan's regional structural stresses, including the area that Dragonfly is set to investigate.

Dragonfly is a NASA New Frontiers mission that will deploy a rotorcraft lander to Titan, the only moon in our Solar System with a dense atmosphere and persistent liquid bodies on its surface. Unlike Earth, Titan's lakes and rivers contain liquid methane and ethane. The mission will explore the region around Selk Crater, where impacts may have introduced water ice and organic materials, potentially creating once-habitable environments. Understanding the area's structural framework will help anticipate what Dragonfly may encounter upon arrival.

Astrophysics Masters Thesis

TESS Light Curve Analysis

As part of my master's thesis at the CUNY Graduate Center, working with Dr. Jackie Faherty and the BDNYC Research Group at the American Museum of Natural History, Department of Astrophysics, I analyzed photometric light curves from NASA's Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (TESS) to measure stellar rotation periods for members of the Carina-Near moving group. Working with 99 targets, I applied the Lomb-Scargle periodogram method to extract rotation periods from TESS high-cadence observations, successfully measuring periods for 10 objects and contributing age constraints for ultracool dwarf companion systems identified through the Backyard Worlds: Planet 9 citizen science project.

This work contributed to a broader effort to characterize the rotational and magnetic properties of young low-mass stars and their companions, with implications for stellar evolution and planetary formation. Results were published as part of Rothermich, A., Faherty, J., et al. (including Cecilio-Flores-Elie, R.) (2024), 89 New Ultracool Dwarf Co-Moving Companions Identified with the Backyard Worlds: Planet 9 Citizen Science Project, The Astronomical Journal.

Planet-Moon Interaction

The second project of my master's thesis investigated the forces driving geological activity on moons in our solar system by analyzing the mass ratios, separation distances, and binding energies of planet-moon systems. Inspired by the diverse surface activity observed on moons like Io, Europa, and Enceladus, I asked whether similar orbital parameters could help identify potentially active and habitable worlds beyond Earth. Building on the work of Faherty et al. (2021) and Rothermich et al. (2024), I extended the analysis from stellar and exoplanetary companions down to moons within our own solar system.

Results showed that geologically active moons: Io, Europa, Ganymede, Titan, and Enceladus, cluster at mass ratios between 10⁻⁸ and 10⁻⁴ and orbital separations between 10⁻⁴ AU and 10⁻² AU, suggesting that internal heating and geological processes concentrate within these ranges. Notably, cold ocean world candidates such as Proxima b, TRAPPIST-1f, and LHS 1140b fall within similar mass ratio ranges, pointing to potential correlations between orbital dynamics and geological or atmospheric activity on these worlds.

Near-Surface Geophysics
in the Yucatán Peninsula

In the summer of 2021, I conducted research in the Yucatán Peninsula as part of the NSF REU "Water Quality in the Yucatán Peninsula" at Northern Illinois University, working with Dr. Philip J. Carpenter and Dr. Melissa Lenczewski.The project focused on the Yucatán Peninsula's vast karstic aquifer system, the primary freshwater source for the region, where I used electrical resistivity tomography with the SuperSting™ to conduct geophysical surveys near cenotes, sinkholes formed by collapsed limestone that expose the underlying groundwater system.

The surveys were designed to characterize subsurface water flow, assess water quality, and evaluate potential anthropogenic impacts from tourism and regional migration. At Agua Azul, I identified previously unmapped water-filled conduits, fracture zones, and an air cavity, features that speak directly to the structural complexity of karst systems and how fluids move through fractured subsurface environments.

This work was presented at the American Geophysical Union (AGU) as an oral presenter and as a poster presenter at the NDiSTEM–SACNAS conferences in 2021 and 2022. It was also my first hands-on experience with the kind of question that now drives my research: how do fluids move through fractures, and what signatures do they leave behind?

Teaching & Outreach

I hold a New York State teaching certification in Childhood Education (Grades 1-6) with a Bilingual Extension. Before pivoting to planetary geophysics, I spent nine years as a second-grade bilingual teacher in the Bronx, teaching all subjects in Spanish and English to students from predominantly low-income, first-generation, and immigrant families, as well as students with disabilities. I developed culturally responsive curricula that drew on students' lived experiences, used scaffolded instruction to support multilingual learners and diverse learning needs, and mentored student-teachers each year.

That work shaped how I explain new ideas, how I think about equity in learning, and why creating spaces for different learning styles matters. At Cornell University, I taught the lab section for EAS 2250 (Earth Systems). The skills I built in the classroom, breaking down complex ideas, meeting students where they are, and making room for curiosity are the same ones I bring into every scientific space I am part of.
